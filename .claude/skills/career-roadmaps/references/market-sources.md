# Market Sources for Career Roadmaps

Reference file for the `career-roadmaps` skill. Use these sources when justifying why a roadmap, specialization, or course is valuable in the medium term (2–5 years) and long term (5–10 years).

**Last reviewed:** 2026-09-26

## How to use this file

1. **Check for newer editions first.** Most of these sources update yearly or faster. Before relying on a summary below, search the web for a newer edition and prefer it if one exists. Update this file when you find one.
2. **Cite the source and its date** whenever a roadmap, course, or career-strategy essay makes a demand claim (e.g. "fast-growing according to BLS 2025–35 projections").
3. **Respect the horizon of each source.** Most forecasts cover ~5 years (to 2030) or ~10 years (to 2035). Anything beyond that is scenario planning, not forecasting. Present long-term claims as scenarios with explicit uncertainty.
4. **Mind the geography.** US, EU, and Latin American demand can diverge significantly. State which market a claim applies to.
5. **Distinguish technical potential from forecasts.** Automation-exposure figures (e.g. McKinsey) describe what *could* be automated, not predicted job losses.
6. **Prefer skills over job titles** when designing courses. Job titles change faster than the underlying skills.

## 1. WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025

- **Publisher:** World Economic Forum
- **Published:** January 2025 (fifth edition of the series)
- **Horizon:** 2025–2030
- **Method:** Survey of 1,000+ employers representing over 14 million workers across 22 industry clusters and 55 economies, complemented by data from partners such as LinkedIn, Coursera, Indeed and ADP.
- **Link:** https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/

**Key findings (summarized):**
- Employers expect around 170 million jobs to be created and 92 million displaced by 2030, a net gain of roughly 78 million, with overall churn equal to about 22% of formal jobs studied.
- Employers expect roughly 39% of core job skills to change by 2030, slightly less disruption than the 2023 edition predicted.
- Technology skills are expected to grow in importance faster than any other category. AI and big data rank first, followed by networks and cybersecurity, then technological literacy.
- Human skills such as creative thinking, resilience, flexibility and agility remain important alongside technical ones.
- The skills gap is cited by employers as the main barrier to business transformation.

**Relevance for roadmaps:** Primary source for ranking which skills to prioritize in courses. Supports including AI/data, cybersecurity, and technological literacy courses across multiple roadmaps.

**Limitations:** Based on employer expectations, not observed data. Global aggregate, so regional patterns may differ.

## 2. WEF: Four Futures for Jobs in the New Economy (AI and Talent in 2030)

- **Publisher:** World Economic Forum
- **Published:** January 2026
- **Horizon:** To 2030, scenario-based
- **Link (summary article):** https://www.weforum.org/stories/2026/01/here-are-four-ways-ais-impact-on-job-markets-might-take-shape/

**Key findings (summarized):**
- Presents four plausible scenarios built on two axes: how fast AI advances and how ready the workforce is.
- In the most accelerated scenario, rapid AI progress plus a well-prepared workforce leads to an AI-centric economy, where many jobs disappear but new ones scale quickly, including roles in which humans orchestrate AI agents.

**Relevance for roadmaps:** A good template for the long-term section of career-strategy essays. Instead of a single prediction, show how a roadmap holds up under different scenarios.

**Limitations:** Scenarios, not forecasts. No probabilities assigned.

## 3. US Bureau of Labor Statistics: Employment Projections

- **Publisher:** US Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS)
- **Latest round:** 2025–2035, released 27 August 2026 (the previous 2024–2034 round was released August 2025)
- **Horizon:** 10 years
- **Links:**
  - Projections home: https://www.bls.gov/emp/
  - 2025–35 news release: https://www.bls.gov/news.release/ecopro.nr0.htm
  - Fastest-growing occupations: https://www.bls.gov/ooh/fastest-growing.htm
  - Employment by detailed occupation: https://www.bls.gov/emp/tables/emp-by-detailed-occupation.htm

**Key findings (summarized):**
- 2025–35 round: total US employment projected to grow by about 5.9 million (3.5%), slower than the 10.9% recorded over 2015–25, mainly driven by private healthcare and social assistance.
  - Computer and mathematical occupations are the fifth fastest-growing major group (about 7.3%); BLS cites the continued spread of digital tools and AI solutions.
  - Data scientists: about 34.6%, the third fastest-growing occupation. Computer and information research scientists: about 21.8%.
  - Information security analysts: about 21%; BLS cites more frequent cyberattacks and the increased use of AI.
  - Software developers: about 10.2%, roughly three times the all-occupation average. Computer programmers: about -7%; BLS notes that AI is being used to automate repetitive programming tasks.
  - Engineering: computer hardware engineers about 9%, electrical engineers about 10%, electronics engineers (except computer) about 4%, industrial engineers about 12% (automation expertise is cited).
  - Energy: solar photovoltaic installers (about 36.5%) and wind turbine service technicians (about 29.5%) are among the five fastest-growing occupations; utilities is the fastest-growing major sector (about 9.8%), with nearly all of that growth expected from electric power generation.
  - Fast percentage growth does not always mean many jobs: some of the fastest-growing occupations are very small in absolute numbers.
- 2024–34 round (for context): overall growth of about 3.1%; information security analysts about 28.5% and data scientists about 33.5%. The 2025–35 round lowered the information security figure.

**Relevance for roadmaps:** Most rigorous occupation-level data for the US. The Occupational Outlook Handbook also lists typical education requirements and median pay, which helps define roadmap entry points and expected outcomes.

**Limitations:** US only. BLS itself notes that point estimates don't capture the uncertainty of long-term projections. Always compare percentage growth with absolute job numbers.

## 4. Cedefop Skills Forecast

- **Publisher:** Cedefop (European Centre for the Development of Vocational Training, an EU agency)
- **Latest release:** 2026 (published July 2026)
- **Horizon:** 2023–2035
- **Geography:** EU-27, plus Iceland, North Macedonia, Norway, Switzerland and Türkiye
- **Links:**
  - Interactive tool: https://www.cedefop.europa.eu/en/tools/skills-forecast
  - 2026 release announcement: https://www.cedefop.europa.eu/en/news/cedefop-updates-skills-forecast-new-data-guide-europes-skills-and-labour-market-policy-2035

**Key findings (summarized):**
- Overall EU-27 employment is projected to grow by just over 4% between 2023 and 2035.
- ICT professionals and technicians are projected to grow about 2.5% per year across the EU, with wide variation by country: around 1.3% in Italy and Germany, up to 7.8% in Romania.
- Electricity generation is among the sectors with the highest projected growth; coal mining and fuel manufacturing decline.
- Strong growth also expected for science and engineering roles requiring advanced digital skills.
- The 2026 release adds analysis of replacement demand (jobs opening up as workers retire), which drives shortages in several sectors.
- The companion Cedefop Labour Skills and Shortage Index (CLSSI) provides occupation-level shortage projections.

**Relevance for roadmaps:** Best source for European demand and for country-level comparison. Replacement demand is useful when arguing that a field will need people even without strong net growth.

**Limitations:** Uses a harmonized methodology for comparability, so national forecasts may be more precise for individual countries.

## 5. McKinsey Global Institute: Agents, Robots, and Us

- **Publisher:** McKinsey Global Institute
- **Published:** November 2025 (US edition); May 2026 (Europe edition)
- **Horizon:** ~5 years, to 2030
- **Links:**
  - Europe edition: https://www.mckinsey.com/mgi/our-research/agents-robots-and-us-how-ai-reshapes-work-and-skills-in-europe
  - US edition (PDF): https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/mckinsey/mckinsey%20global%20institute/our%20research/agents%20robots%20and%20us%20skill%20partnerships%20in%20the%20age%20of%20ai/agents-robots-and-us-skill-partnerships-in-the-age-of-ai.pdf

**Key findings (summarized):**
- Frames future work as a partnership between people, AI agents (non-physical work) and robots (physical work).
- Currently demonstrated technologies could in theory automate activities making up about 57% of US work hours. The authors stress this is technical potential, not a forecast of job losses, and that adoption depends on cost, regulation, integration time and organizational readiness.
- More than 70% of skills employers seek today are used in both automatable and non-automatable work, so most skills remain relevant but are applied differently.
- Introduces a Skill Change Index: digital and information-processing skills are among the most exposed to change; interpersonal skills such as coaching are among the least.
- Demand for "AI fluency" (using and managing AI tools) grew about sevenfold in two years in US job postings, faster than any other skill.
- Value from AI depends on redesigning whole workflows, not just automating individual tasks.

**Relevance for roadmaps:** Justifies including AI fluency and workflow/systems design across roadmaps. The Skill Change Index helps decide which course topics are likely to shift most.

**Limitations:** Consulting-firm analysis; the automation figure is often misquoted as a job-loss forecast.

## 6. Stanford AI Index 2026

- **Publisher:** Stanford Institute for Human-Centered AI (HAI)
- **Published:** 2026 (ninth edition), 400+ pages
- **Horizon:** Retrospective data with trend analysis
- **Links:**
  - Full report: https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report
  - Economy chapter: https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/economy
  - Lightcast labor-market contribution: https://lightcast.io/resources/research/stanford-ai-index-2026

**Key findings (summarized):**
- Job-posting data (via Lightcast) now tracks 10 AI skill clusters and 300+ AI skills. Agentic AI was added as a new cluster.
- Skills such as Agentic AI, AI Agents and LangGraph grew enormously in demand from 2024 to 2025, while chatbot-oriented skills (ChatGPT, Conversational AI) declined.
- Share of job postings mentioning AI skills in 2025: Singapore 4.7%, United States 2.6%, Chile 2.4%, United Kingdom 1.9% (Lightcast page).
- Generative AI is used in at least one business function at about 70% of organizations.
- Reported productivity gains: roughly 14–15% in customer support, 26% in software development, 50% in marketing output; smaller gains in tasks requiring deeper reasoning.
- Emerging evidence suggests heavy reliance on AI may slow long-term skill development.

**Relevance for roadmaps:** Tracks which specific AI skills employers are asking for, useful for keeping course content current. The learning-penalty finding supports designing courses that build fundamentals before relying on AI tools.

**Limitations:** Skill-level demand shifts quickly; specific tools/frameworks named here may be outdated within 1–2 years.

## 7. Anthropic Economic Index

- **Publisher:** Anthropic
- **Latest report:** June 2026 ("Cadences"); earlier reports in January and March 2026
- **Horizon:** Current usage snapshots, released several times per year
- **Links:**
  - Latest report: https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-june-2026-report
  - Dataset (CC-BY): https://huggingface.co/datasets/Anthropic/EconomicIndex

**Key findings (summarized):**
- Tracks how AI is actually used across the economy, mapped to occupational tasks (O*NET).
- March 2026 report: about 49% of jobs had at least a quarter of their tasks performed using Claude; coding tasks are moving from collaborative use toward more automated workflows via the API.
- June 2026 report: introduces a survey linking people's AI use with their expectations about their jobs. Earlier interviews found workers reporting large productivity gains alongside displacement worries, concentrated among early-career workers and occupations with the most AI usage.

**Relevance for roadmaps:** The open dataset can be used programmatically to estimate which tasks within a roadmap's target occupations are most exposed to AI, and which are growing in augmentation (human + AI) rather than automation.

**Limitations:** Reflects usage of one AI provider's products, not the whole market.

## 8. ILO: Panorama Laboral 2025, América Latina y el Caribe

- **Publisher:** International Labour Organization (ILO, OIT in Spanish)
- **Published:** December 2025 (annual series)
- **Horizon:** 2025 data (mostly first half of the year) with a short outlook for 2026
- **Geography:** Latin America and the Caribbean, with country tables that include Chile
- **Method:** Compilation of national labour-force survey data by the ILO regional office.
- **Link (Spanish PDF):** https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2025-12/OIT-Informe-PANORAMA-LABORAL-2025.pdf

**Key findings (summarized):**
- Regional unemployment fell to about 5.8%, its lowest level in recent years, but informality stays close to 47% of workers and youth unemployment is 12.5%.
- Chile: unemployment of 8.8% in the first half of 2025 (8.5% a year earlier), above the regional average; informality below 30%, among the lowest in the region together with Uruguay.
- Regional GDP growth is projected at 2.3% for 2026.
- The report names digitalization, including AI, as a source of opportunities in technology and advanced services and of displacement risk in routine, low-skill occupations.
- The 2025 special topic covers returns to education among workers on web-based platforms.

**Relevance for roadmaps:** Context for the Chile-anchored strategy: a relatively formal labour market with higher unemployment than the regional average. Use it to frame local conditions, not to argue for one specialization over another.

**Limitations:** Macro labour-market data only; no occupation-level or skill-level projections. Chilean figures cover the first half of the year.

## 9. Consejo de Competencias Mineras and Fundación Chile: Fuerza Laboral de la Gran Minería Chilena 2025–2034

- **Publisher:** Alianza CCM-Eleva: the Consejo de Competencias Mineras (CCM) of the Consejo Minero, and the Eleva programme of Fundación Chile
- **Published:** December 2025 (10th edition; the series started in 2011)
- **Horizon:** 2025–2034 (10-year demand projection)
- **Geography:** Chile, large-scale mining (mining companies and their suppliers)
- **Method:** Primary data from 24 participating companies covering 70,250 workers (data cut-off December 2024), plus company investment portfolios. Demand = replacement of retiring workers plus new-project demand.
- **Link (Spanish PDF):** https://ccm-eleva.cl/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Estudio-Fuerza-Laboral-de-la-Gran-Mineria-2025-2034.pdf

**Key findings (summarized):**
- The large-scale mining workforce reached 208,882 people in 2024; about three in four work for suppliers rather than the mining companies themselves.
- Projected demand of 36,895 new workers by 2034, linked to an investment portfolio above US$51 billion. Retirement accounts for 87.4% of that demand.
- Demand is concentrated in Antofagasta (50.1%), Macrozona Centro (20.7%) and Atacama (16.0%).
- By profile: 13,073 maintenance technicians (mechanical 9,257, electromechanical 1,779, electrical 1,680, instrumentation 357), 9,458 operators, 5,255 professionals and 2,399 supervisors.
- Automation: 178 autonomous haul trucks today, projected to reach 550 by 2034; autonomous or teleoperated drills from 52 to 122; teleoperated loaders from 35 to 61. About 1,000 people work in Integrated Operations Centres (CIO) at 15 sites in five regions.
- Digital adoption (sites implementing or piloting): AI at 44% in extraction and processing, 48% in maintenance and 54% in CIOs; cybersecurity at 100% of CIOs and 68% to 84% of other processes; Big Data at 52% to 77%.
- The study concludes that autonomous and digital technologies open new opportunities in advanced maintenance, digital support and technology management.

**Relevance for roadmaps:** The most concrete evidence for the Chilean mining moat: automation, remote operations centres, AI pilots and cybersecurity are real and growing. It also shows where the volume is: most demand is for maintenance technicians and operators, with professional roles a smaller share. Useful for the Edge AI / Physical AI and Control Systems paths (instrumentation, electromechanical maintenance, autonomous fleets, CIO operations) and for OT security.

**Limitations:** Industry-produced study. Technology figures come from 9 technology questionnaires from mining companies, and the text reports shares of sites ("faenas") while the chart labels them as shares of companies. Digital or AI roles are not projected as separate profiles.

## 10. CENIA and ECLAC: Índice Latinoamericano de Inteligencia Artificial (ILIA) 2025

- **Publisher:** National Center for Artificial Intelligence of Chile (CENIA) with the Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (ECLAC / CEPAL), funded by the EU through the EU-LAC Digital Alliance
- **Published:** Presented 3 October 2025 in Santiago (3rd edition); the English key-findings volume on applied AI and human talent is a file dated March 2026
- **Horizon:** Current readiness snapshot; not a forecast
- **Geography:** 19 Latin American and Caribbean countries
- **Method:** Composite index of 100+ sub-indicators in three dimensions (enabling factors, research/development/adoption, governance); talent analysis based on LinkedIn profile data. Not fully comparable with the 2024 edition because of method changes.
- **Links:**
  - ECLAC press release: https://www.cepal.org/en/pressreleases/latin-america-and-caribbean-accelerate-adoption-artificial-intelligence-though
  - Key findings, applied AI and human talent (PDF): https://indicelatam.cl/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/ILIA-2025.-Key-Findings-Applied-Artificial-Intelligence-and-Human-Talent-compressed.pdf

**Key findings (summarized):**
- Chile, Brazil and Uruguay are the "pioneers", each scoring above 60 points.
- Chile has the highest score in the Human Talent subdimension (66.75), the only country above 60; the regional average is 37.32.
- The region produces 6.6% of global GDP but receives 1.12% of global AI investment; ECLAC reports an accelerated brain drain of AI specialists since 2022.
- On LinkedIn data, Chile ranks second in the region (after Costa Rica) in concentration of AI engineering talent, but AI talent is growing more than twice as fast worldwide as in Latin America. In Chile, PyTorch, TensorFlow and Keras emerged among the AI engineering skills listed in 2023–2024.
- The report summarizes a 2024 CENIA study (with SOFOFA, SENCE, the Ministry of Labor and Stanford academics): about 4.7 million people, half of Chile's workforce, could speed up at least 30% of their tasks with generative AI; for software developers the share of tasks is 87%.

**Relevance for roadmaps:** The best regional source for Chile's position in AI: strong relative readiness and talent, weak investment and a talent drain. Supports the Applied AI and Edge AI paths for Chile-based readers, and the case for building skills that travel (remote and DACH markets).

**Limitations:** A readiness index, not a labour-demand forecast. Talent measures depend on LinkedIn profiles. The 4.7 million figure comes from a separate 2024 study reported inside ILIA, and it measures task acceleration potential, not jobs gained or lost.

## 11. ILO and World Bank: Buffer or Bottleneck? Employment Exposure to Generative AI and the Digital Divide in Latin America

- **Publisher:** International Labour Organization and World Bank (ILO Working Paper 121)
- **Published:** 31 July 2024
- **Horizon:** Current exposure; not a forecast of job gains or losses
- **Geography:** Latin America and the Caribbean
- **Method:** Task-based GenAI exposure scores applied to household-survey occupation data, adjusted for access to digital tools at work (PIAAC data for Chile, Ecuador, Mexico and Peru).
- **Links:**
  - Summary: https://www.ilo.org/publications/buffer-or-bottleneck-employment-exposure-generative-ai-and-digital-divide
  - PDF: https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2024-07/WP121_web.pdf

**Key findings (summarized):**
- Between 26% and 38% of jobs in the region could be exposed to generative AI.
- 8% to 14% of jobs have augmentation potential (productivity gains); only 2% to 5% face a risk of full automation.
- About 17 million jobs that could benefit from augmentation are held back by gaps in digital access.
- Among the four countries with PIAAC data, Chile has the largest share of employment in jobs with augmentation potential.

**Relevance for roadmaps:** Supports framing AI in the region as augmentation more than replacement, and treats digital infrastructure as the constraint. Useful background for the Applied AI path and the strategy essay's Chilean context.

**Limitations:** Older than the other sources (2024) and based on GenAI capabilities at that time. A Chile-specific exposure share appears only in a chart and in press coverage, so do not quote a single Chilean figure from it.

## Supporting datasets

- **market-claims-ledger.md** (this folder): the claims actually cited in the roadmaps and the strategy essay, one row each with source, edition, horizon, geography and URL, plus verdicts on claims that were dropped.

- **O*NET OnLine** (https://www.onetonline.org): US occupational database with tasks, skills and technologies per occupation. Useful for mapping courses to concrete job tasks, and it's the taxonomy used by the Anthropic Economic Index.
